## Script

**1 · Cover (Rolf).** Good morning/afternoon! We are [group name], and our
project is "Tingi: The Sari-Sari Store as a Cultural Archive." Today we will
show our theme, how we split the work, our early design work, and our plan
until the final presentation.

**2 · Who does what (Rolf).** Each of us has one main role, but everyone helps
with research and fieldwork. Ethan leads the visual design, Michael builds the
working website, Aezekiel handles research and sources, Elijah writes the
write-up and the stories on the site, and I handle the documentation, the
final PDF and the slides. Next, Aezekiel will introduce our theme.

**3 · Our theme (Aezekiel).** Our theme is the sari-sari store. Almost every
Filipino neighborhood has one, and almost everyone here has bought something
from one. We see it as more than a store: it is where community identity,
everyday economics and neighborhood stories meet. So our website is designed
like a sari-sari store. You look in through the window, browse the shelves,
and each product opens a story. The interface itself acts out the theme. We
are framing it under Cultural Heritage and Public Narratives, and it narrates
community identity.

**4 · Our five stories (Elijah).** Each "product" on our website opens one
story. One: Tingi, buying in small portions, like one sachet of shampoo,
because many families live on a daily budget. Two: Utang, the listahan or
credit notebook, where debt becomes a bond of trust between the tindera and
the neighbors. Three: the Tambayan, the store as the place where neighborhood
news is shared. Four: Signs and labels, the hand-painted signs and loud
packaging, which are a kind of everyday Filipino design. Five, our critical
story: the sachet economy. Buying in sachets costs more per gram than buying
in bulk, big companies profit from it, and the sachets become plastic waste.
So our angle shows both sides: tingi as survival, but also a critical look at
who benefits.

**5 · Symbols we'll analyze (Aezekiel).** For our write-up, we will analyze
four symbols found in every sari-sari store. The rehas, or window grill,
separates the seller from the buyer; it shows both trust and distrust. The
listahan turns debt into a social bond. The "Bawal ang utang, bukas pwede"
signs use humor to cope with hard times. And the tindera, who is often a
mother running a business from home, connects the store to women's work and
livelihood. These connect directly to our course topics: symbolism, public
narratives and criticism.

**6 · Site map (Michael).** We kept the four standard sections from the
instructions, but turned each one into part of the store. Home is the
storefront, seen through the rehas. The Gallery is the shelves, where each
product opens one of our five stories. About is "Tungkol sa Tindahan." And
Contact is "Pabili!", styled like an order slip. We plan to make a working
website, not just a mock-up: designed in Figma, built with HTML, CSS and
JavaScript, and hosted on GitHub Pages.

**7 · Moodboard (Ethan).** This is our early moodboard. These are illustrated
placeholders for now; after fieldwork we will replace them with our own
photos of real sari-sari stores. Sachet strips give us rhythm and color. The
rehas window gives us line and perspective. Hand-painted signs give us
emphasis. Candy jars give us form and shape. The listahan and plywood give us
texture.

**8 · Palette & type (Ethan).** Our colors come straight from the store: dark
brown from the wood, cream from paper, red from the signboards, yellow from
sachet packaging, and teal from the painted walls. For type: Bungee for
headings because it looks like painted signage, Nunito Sans for readable body
text, and Caveat, a handwriting font, for the listahan notes. One palette and
one type system across all pages gives us harmony.

**9 · Homepage wireframe (Michael).** This is our first low-fidelity
wireframe. One: the navigation bar. Two: the store sign, our main point of
emphasis. Three: the rehas window that frames the shelves, giving line and
rhythm. Four: the products; hovering lights one up in yellow, and clicking
opens a story. Five: the counter, where the tindera greets you, and the
footer with credits and sources. Next, we turn this into high-fidelity
screens in Figma.

**10 · Elements & principles (Ethan).** Here is how we will apply every
element and principle required. Line from the grill; color from sachets and
signs; texture from plywood, paint and plastic; perspective from looking in
through the window; form and shape from jars and sachet strips; and space
from packed but organized shelves. Balance from the centered sign, rhythm
from repeated sachets, emphasis from the store sign, harmony from one palette
and type system, and proportion from the big sign against small sachets. In
our final PDF, each will have a captioned screenshot as proof.

**11 · Our timeline (Rolf).** Today is our progress report. November 4 to 7:
research, fieldwork photos, and Figma screens. November 8 to 12: build the
website and write the five stories. November 13 and 14: screenshots,
captions and the write-up. November 15 and 16: compile the PDF, finish the
slides, rehearse. And on [November 17 or 18], our final presentation.

**12 · Challenges & next steps (Elijah).** Three challenges: showing poverty
critically, without romanticizing it; keeping our scope to five strong
stories; and asking store owners' permission before taking photos. Next
steps: all of us photograph two or three local stores, led by Aezekiel;
Aezekiel and I gather sources; Ethan makes the Figma screens; and Michael
starts building the site.

**13 · Salamat po! (Everyone).** Thank you! We would love your feedback,
especially on our theme and our critical angle.
